# What are Rate Limits?

A Rate Limit restricts how many requests a client can make within a specific period.

Examples include:

```text
100 Requests / Minute

--------------------

500 Requests / Hour

--------------------

10,000 Tokens / Minute
```

Rate limits protect services from being overloaded.

---

# Why Do APIs Use Rate Limits?

Imagine one client sends:

```text
100,000 Requests

↓

1 Minute
```

Without limits,

one user could consume all available resources,

making the service unavailable for everyone else.

Rate limits ensure fair usage.

---

# Per-User vs Global Rate Limits

Worth distinguishing, since a multi-tenant AI application (one serving many different users or customers) typically needs to enforce rate limits at more than one level simultaneously.

```text
Per-User (or per-API-key) Rate Limit
→ Limits how much ANY SINGLE user/customer can consume
→ Prevents one noisy or misbehaving user from degrading
  service for everyone else
→ e.g. "each user gets 100 requests/minute"

Global (or system-wide) Rate Limit
→ Limits total throughput across ALL users combined,
  often because it mirrors a limit imposed by an upstream
  provider (like OpenAI's account-level rate limit)
→ e.g. "the whole application can make 5,000 requests/minute
  to the LLM provider, no matter how many users are active"
```

Both matter for different reasons: per-user limits protect fairness _within_ your system, while global limits keep your entire system within what your upstream providers actually allow. A system that only enforces one of these is vulnerable — enforcing only per-user limits could still let combined traffic from many well-behaved users exceed your provider quota; enforcing only a global limit could let one aggressive user consume the entire budget and starve everyone else.

# What is Backpressure?

Backpressure is a mechanism that slows down or temporarily rejects incoming work when the system is approaching its capacity.

Instead of accepting unlimited requests,

the system says:

```text
I'm busy.

Please wait.
```

This protects the entire system from overload.

|Rate Limits|Backpressure|
|---|---|
|Protect external services|Protect your own system|
|Fixed usage limits|Dynamic overload control|
|Often enforced by API providers|Implemented by your application|
|Controls request frequency|Controls system capacity|

Both mechanisms work together.

# Reading Rate-Limit Responses Properly

When a request is rejected with a `429 Too Many Requests`, a well-behaved client shouldn't just guess how long to wait — most APIs include specific response headers to tell you exactly what to do.

```text
Retry-After: 30
→ Wait exactly 30 seconds before retrying, as instructed by
  the server — don't guess

X-RateLimit-Remaining: 0
→ How many requests you have left in the current window
  (0 here means you're out)

X-RateLimit-Reset: 1712345678
→ The timestamp when your limit resets and you can send
  requests again
```

Ignoring these headers and retrying on a fixed, arbitrary schedule is a common mistake — the server is often telling you exactly when it's safe to try again, and using that information directly is more efficient (and more polite to the provider) than blind guessing.

# Exponential Backoff, Concretely — And Why Jitter Matters

The chapter mentions exponential backoff as a technique without showing what it actually does — worth making it concrete, since it's a very common practical interview question.

```text
Attempt 1 fails → wait 1 second → retry
Attempt 2 fails → wait 2 seconds → retry
Attempt 3 fails → wait 4 seconds → retry
Attempt 4 fails → wait 8 seconds → retry
```

Each failure roughly doubles the wait time before the next attempt, rather than retrying immediately or at a fixed interval — this gives an overloaded service progressively more room to recover instead of being hit with an unbroken stream of retries.

There's a refinement worth knowing specifically: **jitter**. If many clients all failed at the same moment (say, the service just came back online after an outage) and all use plain exponential backoff with the exact same formula, they'll all retry at the exact same moments — 1s, 2s, 4s, 8s later, in near-perfect sync — recreating the overload they were trying to avoid. This is sometimes called the **thundering herd problem**.

```text
Plain backoff:        wait = base * 2^attempt
Backoff with jitter:   wait = random(0, base * 2^attempt)
```

Adding randomness ("jitter") spreads retries out over a range instead of a single instant, smoothing the load on recovery instead of causing a synchronized second wave of requests. Production retry logic almost always includes jitter for exactly this reason.

# The Circuit Breaker Pattern, Concretely

Similarly, "circuit breakers" is listed as a technique without explanation — worth walking through its actual three-state mechanism, borrowed directly from electrical circuit breakers.

```text
CLOSED (normal operation)
→ Requests flow through normally
→ Failures are tracked; if failures exceed a threshold
  (e.g. 50% of the last 20 requests failed), trip to OPEN

OPEN (failing fast)
→ Requests are immediately rejected WITHOUT even attempting
  the call — this protects the struggling downstream service
  from getting hit while it's already in trouble, and saves
  the caller from wasting time on calls likely to fail anyway
→ After a cooldown period, transition to HALF-OPEN

HALF-OPEN (cautious testing)
→ A small number of test requests are allowed through
→ If they succeed → transition back to CLOSED (fully recovered)
→ If they fail → transition back to OPEN (not ready yet, wait
  longer)
```

The key insight: a circuit breaker isn't just about retrying smarter — it's about _stopping trying entirely_ for a while once it's clear a downstream service is unhealthy, rather than continuing to hammer it (and waste your own resources) with requests that are very likely to fail anyway.

# Load Shedding: Deciding What to Drop When Overloaded

One more backpressure technique worth a concrete example. When a system truly can't keep up with demand, **load shedding** means deliberately rejecting or dropping some requests to protect the system's ability to serve the rest — rather than trying to serve everyone poorly.

```text
Under heavy load, prioritize:
→ Paying customers over free-tier users
→ Time-sensitive requests over background/batch jobs
→ Requests already deep in a multi-step workflow over
  brand-new requests just starting
```

The idea is to shed load intelligently, by priority, rather than randomly or on a strict first-come-first-served basis that might starve high-value requests behind a flood of low-priority ones. This connects to a broader theme: overload management isn't only about _how much_ to reject, but _which_ requests to reject when rejection becomes unavoidable.

# Best Practices

Respect provider rate limits.

Use exponential backoff **with jitter** when retrying failed requests.

Read and respect `Retry-After` and rate-limit headers instead of guessing retry timing.

Enforce both per-user and global rate limits in multi-tenant systems.

Use circuit breakers to stop calling a failing downstream service entirely for a cooldown period, rather than continuing to retry it.

Queue work instead of rejecting it whenever possible.

When shedding load is unavoidable, do it by priority, not arbitrarily.

Monitor queue length continuously.

Limit concurrent Agent executions.

Protect every external dependency.

# Common Beginner Mistakes

### Mistake 1

Retrying requests immediately after a rate-limit error.

Use exponential backoff with jitter instead — plain backoff without jitter can still cause synchronized retry spikes.

---

### Mistake 2

Ignoring provider quotas.

Always design within documented API limits.

---

### Mistake 3

Accepting unlimited requests.

Every production system has finite capacity.

### Mistake 4

Treating queues as unlimited.

### Mistake 5

### Mistake 6

Enforcing only a global rate limit or only per-user limits, instead of both, in a multi-tenant system.

# Interview Tip

A common interview question is:

> **What is the difference between Rate Limiting and Backpressure?**

A good answer is:

Rate Limiting restricts how frequently requests can be made, typically to protect external services or enforce fair usage. Backpressure protects the application's own infrastructure by slowing, delaying, or rejecting work when the system approaches its processing capacity. Together, they prevent overload and improve production reliability.

---

# Interview Tip

A stronger follow-up worth preparing for:

> **Why is jitter added to exponential backoff, and what problem does a circuit breaker solve that retries alone don't?**

Answer:

Jitter adds randomness to retry timing so that many clients who failed simultaneously don't all retry at the exact same synchronized moments, which would recreate the very overload backoff is trying to avoid — this is the thundering herd problem. A circuit breaker solves a different problem: rather than retrying a failing call repeatedly (even with backoff), it stops attempting calls entirely for a cooldown period once failures cross a threshold, protecting both the struggling downstream service and the caller's own resources, then cautiously tests recovery before resuming normal traffic.

# Key Takeaways

- Rate Limits restrict request frequency; multi-tenant systems typically need both per-user and global limits.
- Backpressure prevents systems from accepting more work than they can handle.
- Well-behaved clients read `Retry-After` and rate-limit headers rather than guessing retry timing.
- Exponential backoff should include jitter to avoid synchronized retry spikes (the thundering herd problem).
- Circuit breakers stop calling a failing service entirely for a cooldown period (open state), rather than continuing to retry it, testing recovery cautiously (half-open) before resuming (closed).
- Load shedding should prioritize which requests to drop, not drop arbitrarily.
- Both rate limits and backpressure are essential for production AI systems.
- Queues, concurrency limits, and exponential backoff are common protection mechanisms.
- Proper overload management improves stability and resilience.
