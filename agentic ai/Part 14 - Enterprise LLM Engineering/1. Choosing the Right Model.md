# Introduction

Imagine you're building an AI Customer Support Agent.

Every user question needs an LLM to generate a response.

Should every request be sent to the most powerful—and most expensive—model?

Probably not.

Consider these two requests.

```text
What are your business hours?
```

```text
Review this 120-page legal contract and identify potential risks.
```

Both require an LLM,

but they do **not** require the same level of intelligence.

Choosing the right model for the right task is one of the most important decisions in Enterprise LLM Engineering.

---

# What is Model Selection?

Model Selection is the process of choosing the most appropriate Large Language Model for a specific task.

Instead of always using one model,

production systems select the model that provides the best balance between:

- Accuracy
- Cost
- Speed
- Context Length
- Reliability

---

# Why is Model Selection Important?

Different models have different strengths.

Some are:

- Fast
- Cheap
- Small

Others are:

- More accurate
- Better at reasoning
- Better at coding
- Better at long-context tasks

Choosing the wrong model may increase cost or reduce response quality.

# Latency vs Throughput

"Speed" actually hides two distinct measurements that can pull in different directions.

```text
Latency
Time to get ONE response back
→ Matters most for interactive, real-time chat

Throughput
How many requests can be processed per second, in total
→ Matters most for batch jobs processing thousands of documents
```

A model optimized for low latency on a single request isn't automatically the best choice for a high-throughput batch pipeline, and vice versa — it's worth being specific about which one actually matters for the task at hand before comparing models on "speed."

# Benchmark Limitations

Public benchmark scores are a common starting point for model selection, but they have real blind spots worth knowing about.

```text
Benchmark Score Looks Great

↓

Does This Benchmark Actually Resemble YOUR Task?

├── Similar domain, similar task format → Somewhat informative

└── Different domain, different task format → Score may not transfer
```

Additional caveats:

```text
Benchmark Contamination
Some benchmark questions may have leaked into a model's training
data, inflating its score without reflecting real capability.

Benchmark Staleness
A model can be fine-tuned specifically to perform well on popular
benchmarks, without a proportional real-world capability gain.
```

This is why "evaluate models using real workloads" (in Best Practices) matters more than benchmark leaderboards — a model's benchmark rank is a hint to investigate, not a substitute for testing on your actual task.

# The Cascade Pattern: Try Cheap First, Escalate If Needed

Rather than picking one fixed model per task type upfront, some systems make the decision dynamically, request by request.

```text
Request Arrives

↓

Try the Small/Cheap Model First

↓

Is the Response Confident and Complete?

├── Yes → Return It (fast, cheap)

└── No  → Escalate to a Larger, More Capable Model
```

```text
Example: Customer Support Query

↓

Small Model Attempts an Answer

↓

Small Model Signals Low Confidence / Can't Resolve It

↓

Escalate to Large Model for a Better Attempt
```

This **cascade pattern** captures much of the cost savings of "always use the cheap model" while still getting large-model quality on the genuinely hard requests that need it — the small model acts as a fast filter, not a hard ceiling on capability.

# Open-Source vs Proprietary Models

Beyond size, the choice between an open-source and a proprietary (API-only) model carries its own trade-offs.

```text
Proprietary Models (via API)
+ Usually strongest raw performance and easiest to start with
+ No infrastructure to manage
− Data leaves your infrastructure (sent to the provider)
− Ongoing per-token cost, less control over updates/versioning

Open-Source Models (self-hosted)
+ Full control over data — nothing leaves your infrastructure
+ No per-token API cost once running (compute cost instead)
− You own the hosting, scaling, and maintenance burden
− Often trails the very best proprietary models on raw capability
```

Organizations with strict data residency or privacy requirements (see the Multi-Tenant chapter's data residency section) sometimes choose open-source, self-hosted models specifically to avoid sending sensitive data to a third-party API, even at some capability cost.

---

# Fine-Tuned vs General-Purpose Models

A general-purpose model can often be adapted to a narrow task more efficiently than always relying on prompting alone.

```text
General-Purpose Model + Detailed Prompting
→ Flexible, works across many tasks, no extra training needed
→ May need a longer prompt with examples to get task-specific quality

Fine-Tuned Model
→ Trained further on examples specific to one narrow task
  (e.g. classifying support tickets into your company's categories)
→ Can be smaller, faster, and cheaper than a general model
  while matching or beating it on that ONE task
```

Fine-tuning is a meaningful investment (data collection, training, evaluation, and ongoing maintenance as needs evolve), so it typically only pays off for a high-volume, narrow, stable task — not for one-off or highly varied requests.

---

# Data Privacy & Licensing Considerations

Model choice isn't purely a performance and cost decision — legal and contractual factors can rule certain models out entirely.

```text
Does the Model Provider's Terms Allow Your Intended Use Case?
(e.g. commercial use, training on outputs, data retention policy)

↓

Does Your Industry Have Data Handling Requirements
That the Provider Can't Meet?
(e.g. healthcare data must stay within certain infrastructure)

↓

If Either Is a Problem → That Model Is Disqualified,
                          Regardless of How Good It Performs
```

These constraints should be checked early — discovering a licensing or compliance blocker after an Agent is already built around a specific model is a costly mistake to unwind.

# Python Example

```python
def choose_model(task):

    if task == "simple":
        return "small-model"

    return "large-model"
```

A version reflecting the cascade pattern:

```python
def answer_with_cascade(query):
    result = small_model.generate(query)

    if result.confidence >= CONFIDENCE_THRESHOLD:
        return result.answer

    # Escalate to a larger model only when needed
    return large_model.generate(query).answer
```

Real systems use more sophisticated routing logic,

but the underlying idea is the same.

# Real-World Example

Imagine an AI Enterprise Assistant.

Requests such as:

- Reset my password
- Summarize today's emails

may use a smaller model.

Requests such as:

- Review this legal agreement
- Generate software architecture
- Analyze financial statements

may use a larger reasoning model.

The platform reduces cost while maintaining quality.

If the assistant serves a healthcare client, its model choice for anything touching patient data is also constrained by which providers can contractually and technically meet healthcare data handling requirements — narrowing the options before cost or speed is even considered.

# Industry Insight

Most enterprise AI platforms do **not** send every request to their largest model.

Instead, they dynamically choose models based on:

- Task complexity
- Cost
- Latency
- Customer requirements

This approach significantly reduces operational costs while maintaining high-quality responses.

Many platforms combine several of the ideas in this chapter at once: a cascade from small to large models, occasional fine-tuned models for high-volume narrow tasks, and a mix of proprietary and self-hosted open-source models depending on each tenant's data requirements.

# Best Practices

Use the smallest model that satisfies the task.

Evaluate models using real workloads, not just public benchmark scores.

Consider a cascade pattern to combine cost savings with large-model quality when needed.

Weigh open-source vs proprietary based on data privacy needs, not just raw capability.

Reserve fine-tuning for high-volume, narrow, stable tasks where it clearly pays off.

Check licensing and compliance constraints before committing to a model.

Monitor cost and latency separately from throughput.

Review model performance regularly.

Avoid using one model for every request.

# Common Beginner Mistakes

### Mistake 1

Using the largest model for every task.

This increases cost without always improving results.

---

### Mistake 2

Choosing models based only on benchmarks.

Real-world performance depends on the application, and benchmarks can be contaminated or not representative of your specific task.

---

### Mistake 3

Ignoring latency.

Fast responses are often more valuable than slightly better responses.

---

### Mistake 4

Never reevaluating model choices.

New models appear frequently and may offer better performance or lower cost.

---

### Mistake 5

Confusing latency and throughput.

Optimizing for one doesn't automatically optimize for the other — check which one your use case actually needs.

---

### Mistake 6

Committing to a model before checking licensing or data privacy constraints.

Discovering a compliance blocker after building around a model is far more costly than checking upfront.

# Interview Tip

A common interview question is:

> **How do production AI systems choose which LLM to use?**

A good answer is:

Production AI systems select models based on factors such as task complexity, accuracy requirements, latency, cost, context length, and reliability. Rather than always using the largest model, they dynamically choose the most appropriate model to balance quality, speed, and operational cost.

A strong follow-up point: mention the **cascade pattern** (try a cheap model first, escalate only when needed), that benchmarks can be misleading without real-workload evaluation, and that data privacy or licensing constraints can rule out a model regardless of its performance.

# Key Takeaways

- Model Selection matches the right LLM to the right task.
- Different models optimize for different trade-offs.
- Latency (per-request speed) and throughput (total capacity) are different metrics that can pull in different directions.
- Benchmark scores can be misleading due to contamination or mismatch with your actual task — real-workload evaluation matters more.
- The cascade pattern escalates from cheap to expensive models only when needed, balancing cost and quality.
- Open-source vs proprietary and fine-tuned vs general-purpose are additional axes of trade-off beyond raw model size.
- Data privacy and licensing constraints can disqualify a model regardless of performance.
- Production systems balance accuracy, latency, and cost.
- Using the largest model for every request is rarely optimal.
- Intelligent model selection is a core capability of Enterprise LLM Engineering.
